# Dating and relationship literature map

Last searched: 12 August 2026. This is a broad evidence map for product discovery, not yet a completed systematic review. Peer review, study design, population, outcome, conflicts, and replication matter more than headline findings. Links below point to publisher, journal, institutional, or full-text records where available.

## 1. Limits of matchmaking prediction

### Finkel et al. (2012) — Online dating: a critical analysis

- Source: *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. [APS overview](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/online-dating.html), [full text](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/eli-finkel/documents/2012_FinkelEastwickKarneyReisSprecher_PSPI.pdf)
- Value: separates the demonstrated benefit of access to candidates from the much weaker evidence behind proprietary compatibility claims. It also warns that extensive profile browsing can encourage objectifying, assessment-oriented choice.
- Limitation: published before swipe apps and modern recommender systems; it cannot evaluate unpublished contemporary systems.
- Product implication: make a narrow claim—OpenMatch assists introductions—and require prospective evidence for every compatibility claim.

### Joel, Eastwick & Finkel (2017) — Is romantic desire predictable?

- Source: *Psychological Science*. [Author manuscript](https://pauleastwick.squarespace.com/s/JoelEastwickFinkel2017PsychScience.pdf)
- Value: machine learning using more than 100 traits/preferences could predict general tendency to desire or be desired, but not the unique desire one particular person would feel for another after meeting.
- Limitation: speed-dating and initial attraction are not long-term partnership.
- Product implication: profile data may support eligibility and user choice, but must not be sold as predicted chemistry.

### Joel et al. (2020) — Predictors of relationship quality across 43 studies

- Source: *PNAS*. [Journal record](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1917036117)
- Value: across 11,196 couples, relationship-specific perceptions were more predictive of current relationship quality than stable individual differences. Prediction of change was much weaker.
- Limitation: largely established couples; many strong predictors describe an existing relationship and cannot select partners beforehand.
- Product implication: do not turn correlates such as perceived commitment or satisfaction into pre-match questionnaires and claim foresight.

### Van Scheppingen et al. (2023) — Personality similarity

- Source: *Journal of Research in Personality*. [Open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10312100/)
- Value: two large couple samples found no evidence that Big Five trait/facet similarity improved life or relationship satisfaction once actor and partner effects were modeled.
- Limitation: existing, primarily different-sex couples and satisfaction outcomes.
- Product implication: no Big Five similarity score in v0.

## 2. Attraction and actual choice

### Eastwick et al. (2023) — Initial compatibility and later relationship initiation

- Source: *PNAS*. [Journal record](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2206925119)
- Value: over 6,600 speed dates and 6,100 follow-ups distinguish consensual desirability, a person’s selectivity, and unique dyadic compatibility. Both general desirability and unique pair impressions predicted later messages and dates.
- Limitation: the useful unique signal appears after an actual interaction; studied speed-dating populations constrain generalization.
- Product implication: optimize access to low-pressure interactions and capture mutual post-interaction interest, rather than pretending profile data contains this signal.

### Zhao et al. (2025) — Stated and revealed mate preferences

- Source: *Social Psychological and Personality Science*. [Journal record](https://doi.org/10.1177/08902070241286254)
- Value: demonstrates why preferences for individual traits can correlate weakly with choices when attraction depends on combinations of traits and opportunity constraints.
- Limitation: specific speed-dating designs and simulations; not evidence that stated boundaries should be ignored.
- Product implication: preserve explicit boundaries; treat soft weights as user decision aids, not stable truths. Never secretly replace them with behavioral inference.

### Hitsch, Hortaçsu & Ariely (2010) — Matching and sorting in online dating

- Source: *American Economic Review*. [AEA record](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.100.1.130)
- Value: large observational market data and matching models illuminate mutual preferences, competition, and assortative patterns.
- Limitation: revealed behavior on one old, heterosexual-oriented platform is shaped by its interface, pool, inequalities, and social conditions; stable matching is not relationship success.
- Product implication: simulate two-sided market effects and do not optimize response rate alone.

### Bruch & Newman (2018) — Aspirational pursuit

- Source: *Science Advances*. [Open manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04840)
- Value: maps messaging markets in four US cities and shows patterned pursuit across a desirability hierarchy.
- Limitation: an inferred hierarchy is descriptive and normatively dangerous; dataset covers heterosexual users and platform behavior.
- Product implication: never display or directly optimize a desirability score; audit concentrated attention and response inequality.

### Pizzato et al. (2013) — Reciprocal recommendation

- Source: *User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction*. [Journal record](https://doi.org/10.1007/S11257-012-9125-0)
- Value: formalizes why person-to-person recommendation differs from recommending products: both sides must prefer the outcome.
- Limitation: interaction and satisfaction proxies do not establish successful relationships.
- Product implication: mutual constraints and two directed explanations are architectural requirements.

## 3. Choice architecture and attention

### D’Angelo & Toma (2017) — Choice overload and reversibility

- Source: *Media Psychology*. [Journal record](https://doi.org/10.1080/15213269.2015.1121827)
- Value: participants choosing from 24 candidates reported lower satisfaction one week later than those choosing from six; reversibility interacted with large sets.
- Limitation: experimental candidate sets and heterosexual sample; effects may depend on goals and context.
- Product implication: finite defaults are reasonable but must be adjustable and prospectively evaluated.

### Pronk & Denissen (2020) — Rejection mindset

- Source: *Social Psychological and Personality Science*. [Journal record](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550619866189)
- Value: repeated profile rejection increased subsequent rejection across studies and related to lower satisfaction with accepted choices.
- Limitation: experimental tasks are not complete dating markets; alternative findings on larger opportunity sets exist.
- Product implication: eliminate infinite swiping and measure whether batch interaction affects selectivity, wellbeing, and useful outcomes.

### Signaling scarcity in dating markets

- Source: *Experimental Economics*, “Propose with a rose?” [Journal record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/abs/propose-with-a-rose-signaling-in-internet-dating-markets/290AAE39D01B06D28169C646242F2788)
- Value: field evidence suggests scarce signals can communicate preference intensity.
- Limitation: scarce signals can become monetized status mechanics and may advantage strategic users; response is not relationship quality.
- Product implication: research a small equal allotment only if users need preference-strength signaling; never sell it.

## 4. Profiles, communication, and moving offline

### Sharabi (2021) — Profiles and first-date communication

- Source: *Communication Monographs*. [Journal record](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2020.1766094)
- Value: experimentally studies how seeing a profile changes first-date communication, uncertainty, satisfaction, and future-interaction desire.
- Limitation: controlled interaction and short-term outcomes.
- Product implication: test which small set of profile information supports conversation without encouraging checklist rejection or false certainty.

### Van der Zanden et al. (2022) — Pictures and text in multimodal profiles

- Source: *Communication Research*. [Open materials/full text](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0093650221995316)
- Value: investigates how users attend to and combine visual and textual profile cues rather than assuming each is independently processed.
- Limitation: impression outcomes do not equal mutual dates or relationships.
- Product implication: profile order is consequential; test photo/context presentation and include accessibility descriptions without beauty ranking.

### Ramirez et al. (2015) — When online partners meet offline

- Source: *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*. [Open article](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/20/1/99/4067565)
- Value: connects amount of pre-meeting online communication, idealization, and face-to-face relational outcomes. Offline meetings frequently function as screening.
- Limitation: observational/self-report, older platform era, and no universal safe timing.
- Product implication: provide optional video/public-date transitions and safety planning, but never pressure users or prescribe a deadline.

### Sharabi & Caughlin (2019) — Text versus video before meeting

- Source: *Media Psychology*. [Article PDF](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/15213269.2019.1648217)
- Value: examines whether richer pre-meeting communication changes idealization and modality-switching outcomes.
- Limitation: controlled conditions and early-stage outcomes.
- Product implication: optional in-app audio/video deserves research before shipping; do not treat more messaging as inherently better.

### BMJ evidence synthesis (2015) — Converting online contact into a first date

- Source: *BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine*. [Open article](https://ebm.bmj.com/content/20/2/48)
- Value: broad meta-narrative synthesis spanning profile, attraction, persuasion, and communication evidence.
- Limitation: combines heterogeneous literatures and predates current apps; some advice is indirect rather than dating-platform field evidence.
- Product implication: useful hypothesis catalogue, not a feature recipe. Validate any prompt/profile intervention directly.

## 5. Relationship process—not prematch compatibility

### Canevello & Crocker (2010) — Responsiveness cycles

- Source: *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. [Open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2891543/)
- Value: dyadic longitudinal studies connect compassionate goals, reciprocal responsiveness, and relationship quality.
- Limitation: roommate/close-relationship development, not an online prematch predictor; causal pathways remain bounded by study design.
- Product implication: optional educational content can encourage respectful responsiveness, but OpenMatch should not score people as “kind” from text.

### Relationship-quality meeting-venue studies

- Sources: Cacioppo et al. (2013), [PNAS open article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3690854/); Hu, Zhu & Zhang (2024), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39058656/)
- Value: asks whether relationships originating online and offline differ in satisfaction or dissolution.
- Limitation: conflicting/small associations, selection and cohort effects, changing platforms, self-report, and potential industry conflicts make causal claims inappropriate.
- Product implication: do not market online meeting as superior; publish OpenMatch-specific longitudinal outcomes with complete caveats.

## 6. Safety, harassment, fraud, and verification

### Gillett (2023) — Dating-app-facilitated sexual harassment

- Source: *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036157/)
- Value: scoping review covers unsolicited sexual images and harassment that crosses from app to offline encounters, while identifying major prevalence and methods gaps.
- Limitation: heterogeneous definitions and insufficient intersectional/prevalence research.
- Product implication: no image messaging at MVP; report pathways must span online and offline harm and publish carefully defined rates.

### Pew Research Center (2023) — User experiences

- Source: [Full report](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2023/02/02/from-looking-for-love-to-swiping-the-field-online-dating-in-the-u-s/)
- Value: large US survey documents mixed outcomes, motives, scams, harassment, and sharply unequal unwanted experiences, including higher reported exposure among LGB users.
- Limitation: US self-report, broad categories, cross-sectional data, no app-level causal conclusions.
- Product implication: stratified safety metrics, participatory design, and relationship-intention clarity are core requirements.

### Shepherd et al. (2023) — Romance fraud systematic review

- Source: *Interacting with Computers*. [Journal article](https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article/35/6/773/7331428), [full text](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/79902042/Shepherd_TaintedLove_Published_2023.pdf)
- Value: synthesizes victimization, offender tactics, detection, intervention, policing, and victim support; stresses adaptive, multi-stage scams and evidence gaps.
- Limitation: inconsistent definitions and limited platform data; proposed ML countermeasures may not be prospectively validated.
- Product implication: build scam response around behavioral friction, human escalation, supported reporting, link/payment warnings, and victim care—not opaque profiling alone.

### Cross (2025) — Romance-scam crime scripts

- Source: *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40923120/)
- Value: highlights nonlinear adaptation and revictimization, challenging simple linear warning models.
- Limitation: scoping synthesis rather than intervention efficacy.
- Product implication: warnings should be contextual and repeatable; support must avoid blaming users and anticipate recontact.

### Social verification and identity checks

- Source: Huang et al. (2013), “Bootstrapping Trust in Online Dating,” [manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.4155)
- Value: an interface study found social verification could increase perceived trust.
- Limitation: perceived trust is not actual safety, the sample was small/crowdsourced, social graphs expose contacts, and verification can create false assurance.
- Product implication: distinguish liveness, photo ownership, age, identity, and safety. Publish exactly what a badge proves. Avoid contact-graph verification in MVP.

### Dhondt et al. (2024) — Location-based dating privacy audit

- Source: USENIX Security. [Paper and artifacts](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity24/presentation/dhondt)
- Value: systematic manual analysis shows mismatches between privacy mental models and app/API disclosure, including exact-location inference in six apps.
- Limitation: 15 apps at a point in time; remediation can change findings.
- Product implication: coarse server-side location, bucketed distance, enumeration resistance, and adversarial location tests are release gates.

### Image-based sexual abuse review

- Source: [PubMed systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37358981/)
- Value: frames nonconsensual image harms as multidimensional violence, not merely objectionable content.
- Limitation: literature has conceptual and methodological weaknesses and is broader than dating apps.
- Product implication: minimize image functionality, restrict downloads/API access, preserve consented report evidence, and create a specialist response procedure.

## 7. Wellbeing and problematic use

### Bonilla-Zorita et al. (2021) — Psychosocial correlates review

- Source: *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*. [Open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7557852/)
- Value: maps motives, user characteristics, self-esteem/body image, risky behavior, harassment, and offline outcomes across 70 studies.
- Limitation: much evidence is cross-sectional and heterogeneous; users differ from nonusers before app use.
- Product implication: do not claim the app causes or cures loneliness; measure wellbeing prospectively and remove appearance/engagement mechanics that lack mission value.

### Problematic online dating systematic review (2025)

- Source: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40608479/), [open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12244275/)
- Value: exposes inconsistent definitions, motive-heavy research, and predominantly cross-sectional designs around compulsive/problematic use.
- Limitation: does not establish that specific app mechanics cause clinical harm.
- Product implication: define anti-compulsion design principles and instrument them without diagnosing users.

### Swiping-app mental-health meta-analysis (2026)

- Source: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42393202/)
- Value: 27 studies and 21,263 participants; differentiates outcomes and reports elevated appearance/body-related vulnerability among sexual-minority participants, while general-wellbeing effects were small/non-significant.
- Limitation: mostly observational evidence and user/nonuser selection; very recent review needs independent appraisal.
- Product implication: avoid causal alarmism but track body image/self-worth impacts in opt-in research and avoid swipe/beauty-ranking UI.

## 8. LGBTQIA+, privacy, and disclosure

### MacLeod & McArthur (2023) — Sexual-minority women review

- Source: *Technology, Mind, and Behavior*. [Journal record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958822000720)
- Value: synthesizes benefits, identity signaling, exclusion, deception, harassment, safety strategies, and hesitation to meet among sexual-minority women.
- Limitation: literature is uneven across identities, geographies, race, and trans inclusion.
- Product implication: identity, eligibility, and visibility must be separate controls; safety cannot assume heterosexual matching dynamics.

### Li et al. (2024) — Closeted users’ privacy

- Source: *Proceedings on Privacy Enhancing Technologies*. [Open paper](https://petsymposium.org/popets/2024/popets-2024-0046.php)
- Value: documents inadvertent outing, institutional/ad-targeting fears, unauthorized photo sharing, extortion, and harassment.
- Limitation: qualitative US sample; risks differ by social/legal context.
- Product implication: no ad ecosystem or contact discovery; offer incognito modes, granular photo/identity disclosure, neutral notifications, and rapid conceal/delete controls.

### LGBTQ+ relationship-initiation study

- Source: Sharabi et al., “A Space of Our Own.” [Institutional record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/a-space-of-our-own-exploring-the-relationship-initiation-experien/)
- Value: mixed-methods evidence from 231 LGBTQIA+ US users emphasizes that relationship initiation and platform adoption vary within—not merely between—identity groups.
- Limitation: US self-selected sample and broad umbrella categories.
- Product implication: never model LGBTQIA+ users as one market side; conduct identity-specific participatory testing.

## 9. Disability, accessibility, and age

### Liddiard et al. (2023) — Online dating and disability scoping review

- Source: *Sexuality and Disability*. [Record](https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/doi%3A10.1007/s11195-022-09771-x?id=ebsco%3Adoi%3A10.1007%2Fs11195-022-09771-x&sid=ebsco%3Aplink%3Acrawler)
- Value: 15 peer-reviewed and 51 grey-literature sources cover participation barriers, disclosure, accessibility, security, and education.
- Limitation: sparse, heterogeneous evidence and many disability experiences remain underrepresented.
- Product implication: accessibility is not only WCAG; disclosure timing, fetishization reporting, accessible-date needs, and non-stigmatizing representation require co-design.

### Digital sexual fields and intellectual disability

- Source: *Cyberpsychology*. [Open article](https://doi.org/10.5817/CP2022-2-6)
- Value: identifies digital access, surveillance/control, literacy, stigma, disclosure expectations, and autonomy issues.
- Limitation: small qualitative population and legal contexts differ.
- Product implication: use plain-language consent and safety material, supported decision-making, and avoid paternalistic exclusion while meeting adult-capacity law.

### Older adults’ privacy and security

- Source: Noah et al. (2024), “Aging Safely Online.” [Journal record](https://doi.org/10.1177/10711813241262022)
- Value: focuses on privacy/security mental models and practices among people 65+, a population often discussed mainly as scam targets.
- Limitation: emerging, limited evidence base.
- Product implication: include older adults in usability/scam studies; never rely on dense icons, hidden gestures, or jargon-heavy warnings.

## 10. Fairness, discrimination, and market health

### Popularity bias in recommender systems

- Source: Abdollahpouri et al. (2019). [Manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05755)
- Value: distinguishes fairness to consumers from supplier/exposure fairness and shows how popular items dominate recommendation.
- Limitation: much evidence comes from item recommendation; people cannot be ethically treated as inventory.
- Product implication: measure exposure opportunity without framing humans as products or promising equal romantic outcomes.

### Fair reciprocal recommendation

- Sources: Chen, Hsieh & Lin, field work on two-sided matching, [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3718920); Neve & Palomares (2019), WE-Rec, [journal record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0950705119303326)
- Value: explores reducing recommendation inequality while maintaining match opportunity in two-sided systems.
- Limitation: fairness objectives encode contestable values; interaction data inherit historical exposure and discrimination.
- Product implication: define fairness publicly with affected communities, compare multiple metrics, and avoid learning directly from biased accept/reject logs.

### Race and intersectional experience

- Source: Mason & Tabor (2025), Black women’s pursuit of Black love. [Journal record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211695825000303)
- Value: intersectional analysis shows how platform design and algorithms structure agency and reproduce social inequities.
- Limitation: qualitative/critical evidence does not prescribe one mathematical remedy.
- Product implication: audit the entire opportunity pipeline, avoid race-based ranking, and give affected groups governance power rather than imposing a purely technical fairness fix.

## 11. Evidence gaps most valuable to OpenMatch

1. Prospective prediction of **mutually wanted second dates**, not likes or replies.
2. Longitudinal, diverse, out-of-sample evidence for prematch factors.
3. Comparisons of transparent eligibility/random baselines against reciprocal scores.
4. Effects of finite batches across population, density, orientation, and intention.
5. Safety-feature effectiveness—not adoption or perceived safety alone.
6. Verification’s actual reduction in harm versus exclusion, biometric risk, and false confidence.
7. Outcome and exposure fairness in non-binary, many-to-many matching markets.
8. Accessible and safe disclosure controls for closeted, trans, disabled, and geographically vulnerable users.
9. Scam interventions tested at real decision moments, including victim support and revictimization.
10. Effects of profile ordering, photos, prompts, and video on mutual dates rather than impressions.
11. Healthy-exit measurement with severe missing-not-at-random attrition.
12. Governance mechanisms that keep a nonprofit recommender aligned over decades.

## 12. Research interpretation rules

- Attraction, reply, match, date, second date, relationship, satisfaction, duration, safety, and wellbeing are different outcomes.
- Existing couples cannot reveal which prematch algorithm would have formed good counterfactual couples.
- Correlation among couples may reflect availability, social sorting, discrimination, or relationship formation—not compatibility benefit.
- “Users prefer” requires representative evidence; app reviews and current users exclude nonusers and people who left.
- A statistically significant effect is not automatically large, useful, causal, equitable, or replicable.
- Null findings and subgroup uncertainty must remain visible.
- Protected-group outcome differences must prompt investigation, not automatic use of protected attributes in ranking.
